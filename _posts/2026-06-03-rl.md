    <h2 id="overview">Project Overview</h2>
    <p>
        At its core, this project explores applied RL for training agents to master Laat game.
        The system integrates a game simulator designed specifically for reinforcement learning,
        a Gymnasium environment that enforces strict legal-action masking. The training architecture supports
        maskable Proximal Policy Optimization, behavior cloning derived from synthetic teacher data, and continuous
        fine-tuning from those
        behavior-cloned weights. Agents are trained against dynamic league opponents featuring multiple bot
        styles and are evaluated comprehensively beyond simple win rates. Over the course of development,
        multiple models were produced. Model Three currently serves as most robust reference, while Model
        Two provides the strongest discipline regarding high-card strategies. Model Four acts as an experimental
        checkpoint focused on maximizing win rates through synthetic data.
    </p>

    <h2 id="game">Understanding the Game of Laat</h2>
    <p>
        Laat is played with a standard fifty-two card deck comprising four suits and thirteen ranks per suit,
        completely excluding jokers. In this game, Aces hold the highest value. The fundamental objective is to
        avoid being left with the most cards. A proficient player strives to quickly empty their hand while
        simultaneously forcing opponents to accumulate cards.
    </p>
    <p>
        The turn structure is intuitive yet deeply strategic. After the deck is shuffled and dealt, the player
        holding the starting card, typically the Ace of Spades, begins the game. Each subsequent sub-round
        functions similarly to a trick. A player leads a card, establishing a lead suit that other players must
        follow if possible. If a player cannot follow the lead suit, they are compelled to play any legal card,
        an action formally known as giving laat. When this occurs, the player holding the current highest card
        in the lead suit collects the entire trick, including the laat card, and subsequently starts the next
        sub-round. Conversely, if everyone follows the lead suit cleanly, the highest card merely wins the
        trick, and those cards are moved safely to the discard pile.
    </p>
    <p>
        The true strategic tension in Laat lies in the delicate balance of applied pressure and hidden
        information. Collecting cards is highly detrimental, whereas emptying the hand is the ultimate goal.
        Leading a suit that an opponent lacks forces them to give laat, often resulting in the current highest
        card holder being penalized with the trick. Consequently, players frequently choose to give high cards
        as laat to eliminate dangerous assets from their hands, while preserving lower cards to guarantee safer
        choices in future rounds. Furthermore, giving laat provides public knowledge that the player lacks the
        lead suit. Because the game involves hidden hands, the reinforcement learning agent must navigate these
        decisions based solely on public information and its own cards.
    </p>

    <h2 id="architecture"><span class="header-num">1</span> Architecture and Implementation</h2>
    <p>
        The game is modeled as a <strong>Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP)</strong>,
        which we simplify into a <strong>Masked MDP</strong> during training by encoding the observable history
        and using legal action masks.
    </p>
    <p>
        A standard POMDP is defined by the tuple
        \( \langle \mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, \mathcal{T}, \mathcal{R},
        \Omega, \mathcal{O}, \gamma \rangle \):
    </p>

    <ul>
        <li>\( \mathcal{S} \) is the set of complete hidden states.</li>
        <li>\( \mathcal{A} = \{0,1,\dots,51\} \) is the action space.</li>
        <li>\( \mathcal{T}(s' \mid s, a) \) is the transition function.</li>
        <li>\( \mathcal{R}(s,a) \) is the reward function.</li>
        <li>\( \Omega \) is the observation space.</li>
        <li>\( \mathcal{O}(o \mid s', a) \) is the observation function.</li>
        <li>\( \gamma \in [0,1) \) is the discount factor.</li>
    </ul>

    <h3 id="action-masking"><span class="header-num">1.1</span> Action Masking Formulation</h3>
    <p>
        In standard RL, the policy \( \pi(a \mid o) \) generates a probability
        distribution over the entire discrete action space \( \mathcal{A} \).
        In Laat, most actions are illegal in any given turn due to the strict
        game rules (e.g., following the lead suit). To prevent the policy from
        wasting exploration on illegal moves, we apply
        <strong>Legal Action Masking</strong>.
    </p>
    <p>
        Let \( \mathcal{M}(s) \in \{0,1\}^{|\mathcal{A}|} \) be the legal action
        mask vector for a state \( s \), defined for each card id
        \( a \in \mathcal{A} \) as:
    </p>

    <div class="equation">
        \[
        \mathcal{M}_a(s)=
        \begin{cases}
        1 & \text{if card } a \text{ is a legal move} \\
        0 & \text{otherwise}
        \end{cases}
        \]
    </div>

    <p>
        Let \( f_\theta(o) \in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{A}|} \) be the raw output
        logits of the policy neural network parameterised by \( \theta \)
        given observation \( o \). The masked logits
        \( \tilde{f}_\theta(o) \) are computed by setting the logits of
        illegal actions to a large negative number:
    </p>

    <div class="equation">
        \[
        \tilde{f}_\theta(o)_a=
        \begin{cases}
        f_\theta(o)_a & \text{if } \mathcal{M}_a(s)=1 \\
        -10^9 & \text{if } \mathcal{M}_a(s)=0
        \end{cases}
        \]
    </div>

    <p>
        The action probability distribution
        \( \pi_\theta(a \mid o) \) is then computed using the masked softmax:
    </p>

    <div class="equation">
        \[
        \pi_\theta(a \mid o)
        =
        \text{softmax}(\tilde{f}_\theta(o))_a
        =
        \frac{
        \exp\left(\tilde{f}_\theta(o)_a\right)
        }{
        \sum_{b \in \mathcal{A}}
        \exp\left(\tilde{f}_\theta(o)_b\right)
        }
        \]
    </div>

    <p>
        For any illegal action \( a \) where
        \( \mathcal{M}_a(s)=0 \):
    </p>

    <div class="equation">
        \[
        \pi_\theta(a \mid o) \approx 0
        \]
    </div>

    <p>
        This formulation guarantees that the agent only samples legal actions,
        allowing the neural network to focus all learning capacity on
        strategically optimizing among legal moves.
    </p>

    <h3 id="card-representation"><span class="header-num">1.2</span> Representation of Cards</h3>
    <p>
        Each of the 52 cards is indexed by a unique integer
        \( a \in \{0, \dots, 51\} \).
        The suit \( S(a) \) and rank \( R(a) \) of a card \( a \)
        are determined by the formulas:
    </p>

    <div class="equation">
        \[
        S(a) = \lfloor a / 13 \rfloor
        \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\}
        \quad
        \text{(Spades, Hearts, Diamonds, Clubs)}
        \]
    </div>

    <div class="equation">
        \[
        R(a) = a \bmod 13
        \in \{0, \dots, 12\}
        \quad
        \text{(2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, Jack, Queen, King, Ace)}
        \]
    </div>

    <h2 id="policy"><span class="header-num">2</span> Policy Architectures</h2>
    <p>
        In card games, players take turns sequentially. However, RL models expect a standard input-output loop:
        <em>observe state</em> &rarr; <em>choose action</em> &rarr; <em>get reward</em>.
    </p>
    <p>
        To handle this, the environment uses an asynchronous execution loop:
    </p>

    <ul>
        <li>
            <strong>Fast-Forwarding:</strong>
            When a game starts, the engine shuffles and deals cards. If the game starts with Player 2's turn,
            the environment runs Player 2's and Player 3's turns automatically behind the scenes using their
            respective bot behaviors.
        </li>
        <li>
            <strong>The "Step":</strong>
            The moment it becomes Player 0's turn (the RL agent), the game pauses. The agent is shown the
            observation (its hand, what's on the table, etc.) and it chooses a card to play.
        </li>
        <li>
            <strong>Opponent Simulation:</strong>
            Once Player 0 plays a card, the game resumes, running all subsequent opponent turns and resolving
            tricks/rounds until it is Player 0's turn again (or the game ends).
        </li>
    </ul>

    <p>
        So, to the RL agent, the game feels like a single-player game where it plays a card, the world instantly
        updates, and it's its turn again.
    </p>
    <p>
        Across the training stages, two main neural network architectures are employed:
        a <strong>Behavior Cloning (BC) Classifier</strong> and a
        <strong>Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) Actor-Critic Network</strong>.
    </p>

    <h3 id="bc-arch"><span class="header-num">2.1</span> Behavior Cloning (BC) Architecture</h3>
    <p>
        The BC network is a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) mapping the
        414-dimensional observation vector to 52 action logits:
    </p>

    <figure>
        <img src="{{ '/assets/images/rl/bc_clone.png' | relative_url }}" class="BC-architecture" alt="BC Architecture">
        <figcaption> Behavior Cloning Architecture </figcaption>
    </figure>

    <p>
        The mathematical formulation for the BC forward pass is:
    </p>

    <div class="equation">
        \[
        \mathbf{h}_1 = \tanh(\mathbf{W}_1 \mathbf{o} + \mathbf{b}_1)
        \]
    </div>

    <div class="equation">
        \[
        \mathbf{h}_2 = \tanh(\mathbf{W}_2 \mathbf{h}_1 + \mathbf{b}_2)
        \]
    </div>

    <div class="equation">
        \[
        \mathbf{z} = \mathbf{W}_3 \mathbf{h}_2 + \mathbf{b}_3
        \]
    </div>

    <p>
        where
        \( \mathbf{o} \in \mathbb{R}^{414} \),
        \( \mathbf{W}_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{256 \times 414} \),
        \( \mathbf{b}_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{256} \),
        \( \mathbf{W}_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{256 \times 256} \),
        \( \mathbf{b}_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{256} \),
        \( \mathbf{W}_3 \in \mathbb{R}^{52 \times 256} \),
        and
        \( \mathbf{b}_3 \in \mathbb{R}^{52} \).
    </p>

    <h3 id="ppo-arch"><span class="header-num">2.2</span> Maskable PPO Actor-Critic Architecture</h3>
    <p>
        The reinforcement learning agent uses a dual-headed MLP actor-critic architecture.
        The policy head (Actor) outputs a probability distribution over actions, and the
        value head (Critic) outputs a scalar state-value estimate:
    </p>

    <figure>
        <img src="{{ '/assets/images/rl/ppo.png' | relative_url }}" alt="PPO Architecture">
        <figcaption> Proximal Policy Optimization Architecture </figcaption>
    </figure>

    <p>
        In the standard <code>MlpPolicy</code> configuration for
        <code>sb3-contrib</code>, the actor and critic networks have completely
        independent feature extractors. This allows each head to extract features
        tailored to its specific task: the Actor learns action probability mapping
        (policy), while the Critic learns state quality evaluation (value).
    </p>

    <h3>Formulation</h3>
    <p><strong>Actor:</strong></p>

    <div class="equation">
        \[
        \mathbf{h}_1^{\text{act}} =
        \tanh(\mathbf{W}_1^{\text{act}}\mathbf{o} +
        \mathbf{b}_1^{\text{act}})
        \]
    </div>

    <div class="equation">
        \[
        \mathbf{h}_2^{\text{act}} =
        \tanh(\mathbf{W}_2^{\text{act}}\mathbf{h}_1^{\text{act}} +
        \mathbf{b}_2^{\text{act}})
        \]
    </div>

    <div class="equation">
        \[
        \mathbf{z}^{\text{act}} =
        \mathbf{W}_3^{\text{act}}\mathbf{h}_2^{\text{act}} +
        \mathbf{b}_3^{\text{act}}
        \]
    </div>

    <p><strong>Where:</strong></p>
    <ul>
        <li>
            \(\mathbf{o} \in \mathbb{R}^{414}\) is the input observation vector.
        </li>
        <li>
            \(\mathbf{W}_1^{\text{act}} \in \mathbb{R}^{256 \times 414}\) and
            \(\mathbf{b}_1^{\text{act}} \in \mathbb{R}^{256}\).
        </li>
        <li>
            \(\mathbf{W}_2^{\text{act}} \in \mathbb{R}^{256 \times 256}\) and
            \(\mathbf{b}_2^{\text{act}} \in \mathbb{R}^{256}\).
        </li>
        <li>
            \(\mathbf{W}_3^{\text{act}} \in \mathbb{R}^{52 \times 256}\) and
            \(\mathbf{b}_3^{\text{act}} \in \mathbb{R}^{52}\), producing raw logits
            for the 52 possible card choices, which are masked during inference.
        </li>
    </ul>

    <p><strong>Critic:</strong></p>

    <div class="equation">
        \[
        \mathbf{h}_1^{\text{crit}} =
        \tanh(\mathbf{W}_1^{\text{crit}}\mathbf{o} +
        \mathbf{b}_1^{\text{crit}})
        \]
    </div>

    <div class="equation">
        \[
        \mathbf{h}_2^{\text{crit}} =
        \tanh(\mathbf{W}_2^{\text{crit}}\mathbf{h}_1^{\text{crit}} +
        \mathbf{b}_2^{\text{crit}})
        \]
    </div>

    <div class="equation">
        \[
        V(\mathbf{o}) =
        \mathbf{W}_3^{\text{crit}}\mathbf{h}_2^{\text{crit}} +
        b_3^{\text{crit}}
        \]
    </div>

    <p><strong>Where:</strong></p>
    <ul>
        <li>
            \(\mathbf{W}_1^{\text{crit}} \in \mathbb{R}^{256 \times 414}\) and
            \(\mathbf{b}_1^{\text{crit}} \in \mathbb{R}^{256}\).
        </li>
        <li>
            \(\mathbf{W}_2^{\text{crit}} \in \mathbb{R}^{256 \times 256}\) and
            \(\mathbf{b}_2^{\text{crit}} \in \mathbb{R}^{256}\).
        </li>
        <li>
            \(\mathbf{W}_3^{\text{crit}} \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times 256}\) and
            \(b_3^{\text{crit}} \in \mathbb{R}\), producing a single scalar value
            representing the expected future reward from the current state.
        </li>
    </ul>

    <h3 id="weight-transfer"><span class="header-num">2.3</span> Weight Transfer (BC to PPO Initialization)</h3>
    <p>
        To kickstart PPO training, the pretrained weights from a successfully converged
        BC model are copied into the PPO model:
    </p>

    <ol>
        <li>
            Actor weights
            (\( \mathbf{W}_1^{\text{act}} \),
            \( \mathbf{b}_1^{\text{act}} \),
            \( \mathbf{W}_2^{\text{act}} \),
            \( \mathbf{b}_2^{\text{act}} \))
            are initialized from BC weights
            (\( \mathbf{W}_1 \),
            \( \mathbf{b}_1 \),
            \( \mathbf{W}_2 \),
            \( \mathbf{b}_2 \)).
        </li>
        <li>
            Critic weights
            (\( \mathbf{W}_1^{\text{crit}} \),
            \( \mathbf{b}_1^{\text{crit}} \),
            \( \mathbf{W}_2^{\text{crit}} \),
            \( \mathbf{b}_2^{\text{crit}} \))
            are also initialized from BC weights
            (\( \mathbf{W}_1 \),
            \( \mathbf{b}_1 \),
            \( \mathbf{W}_2 \),
            \( \mathbf{b}_2 \))
            to provide the value network with immediate high-quality feature extraction.
        </li>
        <li>
            The PPO action head
            (\( \mathbf{W}_3^{\text{act}} \),
            \( \mathbf{b}_3^{\text{act}} \))
            is initialized from the BC action head
            (\( \mathbf{W}_3 \),
            \( \mathbf{b}_3 \)).
        </li>
        <li>
            The PPO scalar value head
            (\( \mathbf{W}_3^{\text{crit}} \),
            \( b_3^{\text{crit}} \))
            is initialized randomly.
        </li>
    </ol>

    <h3 id="perspective"><span class="header-num">3.2</span> Perspective Rotation Mechanics</h3>
    <p>
        During learning, the agent is always assigned as Player 0. To evaluate checkpoints or run league training
        effectively from any seating position, we rotate the observation vector relative to the acting player.
        Let <em>p<sub>perspective</sub></em> be the absolute index of the player for whom the observation is
        being encoded, and <em>p<sub>absolute</sub></em> be the absolute index of any player in the game.
        In a game with <em>K</em> active players, the relative player index <em>p<sub>relative</sub></em> is calculated
        as:
    </p>

    <div class="equation">
        \[
        p_{\text{relative}} = (p_{\text{absolute}} - p_{\text{perspective}}) \bmod K
        \]
    </div>

    <p>
        For index values where <code>p<sub>relative</sub> &ge; K</code>, features in the observation array
        default to <code>0.0</code> (as the maximum player capacity is 6, but games usually contain 4 players).
        This rotation mapping is recursively applied to the following observation components:
    </p>

    <ol>
        <li><strong>Current Trick Ownership</strong>: Stored relative to <em>p<sub>perspective</sub></em>.</li>
        <li>
            <strong>Player Hand Counts</strong>: Reordered such that:
            <ul>
                <li>Index <code>0</code> = <code>len(hand[p<sub>perspective</sub>]) / 52.0</code></li>
                <li>Index <code>1</code> = Player <code>(p<sub>perspective</sub> + 1) mod K</code></li>
                <li>Subsequent indices follow in clockwise player order relative to <em>p<sub>perspective</sub></em>.
                </li>
            </ul>
        </li>
        <li><strong>Public Suit Failures</strong>: Reordered using relative player indices.</li>
        <li><strong>Current Player One-Hot</strong>: Rotated according to <em>p<sub>relative</sub></em>.</li>
        <li><strong>Highest Player One-Hot</strong>: Rotated according to the relative index of the current winning
            player.</li>
    </ol>

    <h2 id="exec-loop"><span class="header-num">4</span> The Reinforcement Learning Execution Loop</h2>
    <p>
        The training pipeline operates on a sequential execution loop inside the
        Gymnasium wrapper <code>LaatCardEnv</code>. Because only one player
        (<strong>Player 0</strong>) is the training agent, the environment handles
        all opponent bot actions internally.
    </p>

    <div class="flow-diagram">
        <div class="flow-step">
            <div class="step-number">1</div>
            <div class="step-content">
                <h4>Environment Reset</h4>
                <p><code>PPO Agent → LaatCardEnv.reset(seed)</code></p>
                <p><code>LaatCardEnv → engine.create_game(config)</code></p>
                <p>
                    Cards are shuffled and dealt. The player holding the starting card
                    becomes the active player.
                </p>
            </div>
        </div>

        <div class="arrow">↓</div>

        <div class="flow-step">
            <div class="step-number">2</div>
            <div class="step-content">
                <h4>Advance Until Agent Turn</h4>
                <p>
                    The environment repeatedly queries opponent bots and applies their
                    moves internally:
                </p>
                <ul>
                    <li><code>opponents.choose_action(state)</code></li>
                    <li><code>engine.apply_move(state, card_id)</code></li>
                </ul>
            </div>
        </div>

        <div class="arrow">↓</div>

        <div class="flow-step">
            <div class="step-number">3</div>
            <div class="step-content">
                <h4>Observation Returned</h4>
                <p>The environment returns:</p>
                <ul>
                    <li>Observation vector (<code>414</code>)</li>
                    <li>Action mask (<code>52</code>)</li>
                </ul>
            </div>
        </div>

        <div class="arrow">↓</div>

        <div class="flow-step">
            <div class="step-number">4</div>
            <div class="step-content">
                <h4>Agent Action</h4>
                <p>PPO selects a legal card and calls:</p>
                <p><code>step(action)</code></p>
                <p>
                    The action is immediately applied to the game state through
                    <code>engine.apply_move()</code>.
                </p>
            </div>
        </div>

        <div class="arrow">↓</div>

        <div class="flow-step">
            <div class="step-number">5</div>
            <div class="step-content">
                <h4>Opponent Resolution</h4>
                <p>The environment automatically executes all intermediate opponent turns.</p>
                <p>This continues until either:</p>
                <ul>
                    <li>Player 0 acts again, or</li>
                    <li>The game ends.</li>
                </ul>
            </div>
        </div>

        <div class="arrow">↓</div>

        <div class="flow-step">
            <div class="step-number">6</div>
            <div class="step-content">
                <h4>Reward Calculation</h4>
                <p>Reward <code>R<sub>t</sub></code> is computed from:</p>
                <ul>
                    <li>Intermediate shaping rewards</li>
                    <li>Trick outcomes</li>
                    <li>Terminal game outcomes</li>
                </ul>
            </div>
        </div>

        <div class="arrow">↓</div>

        <div class="flow-step">
            <div class="step-number">7</div>
            <div class="step-content">
                <h4>Transition Returned</h4>
                <p>The environment returns:</p>
                <pre>observation, reward, terminated, truncated, info</pre>
                <p>PPO stores the transition and proceeds to the next step.</p>
            </div>
        </div>
    </div>

    <h2 id="training"><span class="header-num">5</span> Training the Agents</h2>

    <h3 id="pipeline"><span class="header-num">5.1</span> Training Pipeline</h3>
    <p>
        The training pipeline for the Laat agent follows a structured multi-stage progression designed to
        transition the model from synthetic mimicry to strategic reinforcement learning:
    </p>

    <div class="diagram-container">
        <div class="diagram-title">Agent Training Pipeline</div>
        <div class="pipeline">
            <div class="pipeline-step" style="background-color: #9b59b6;">
                1. Synthetic Data Generation
                <div style="font-size: 0.8em; margin-top: 5px;">From heuristic & teachers</div>
            </div>
            <div class="pipeline-arrow">➔</div>
            <div class="pipeline-step" style="background-color: #3498db;">
                2. Behavior Cloning (BC)
                <div style="font-size: 0.8em; margin-top: 5px;">Masked Cross-Entropy Loss</div>
            </div>
            <div class="pipeline-arrow">➔</div>
            <div class="pipeline-step" style="background-color: #e67e22;">
                3. Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
                <div style="font-size: 0.8em; margin-top: 5px;">League Training & Fine-Tuning</div>
            </div>
        </div>
    </div>

    <p>
        First, the agent undergoes <strong>Behavior Cloning (BC)</strong> by training on a dataset of synthetic
        game decisions. The BC network is supervised using a masked cross-entropy loss to predict the exact
        actions taken by heuristic or checkpoint teacher models. After the BC classifier converges, its policy
        weights are transferred to the actor-critic layers of a <strong>Maskable PPO</strong> model. This
        initialization serves as a warm start, allowing the reinforcement learning phase to begin with a policy
        that already understands the rules of the game and can execute basic strategies.
    </p>

    <h3 id="reward"><span class="header-num">5.2</span> Reward Function Calibration</h3>
    <p>
        The environment's reward function is carefully structured to shape strategic decisions while
        prioritizing the ultimate objective of winning the game (ending with the absolute fewest cards). The
        step reward \( \mathcal{R}_t \) is calculated dynamically at each step and depends directly on game
        state transitions:
    </p>

    <div class="equation">
        \[
        \mathcal{R}_t = \mathcal{R}_{\Delta H} + \mathcal{R}_{\text{laat}} + \mathcal{R}_{\text{terminal}}
        \]
    </div>

    <p>
        The individual components of this reward function are mathematically calibrated as follows:
    </p>

    <ul>
        <li>
            <strong>Hand Deltas (\( \mathcal{R}_{\Delta H} \)):</strong> Encourages card reduction. Let \( H_t
            \) be the agent's hand size at step \( t \), and let \( \Delta H_t = H_{t} - H_{t+1} \) be the
            change in hand size:
            <div class="equation">
                \[
                \mathcal{R}_{\Delta H} = \begin{cases}
                0.01 \cdot \Delta H_t & \text{if } \Delta H_t > 0 \quad \text{(Agent discarded cards)} \\
                0.02 \cdot \Delta H_t & \text{if } \Delta H_t < 0 \quad \text{(Agent collected cards)} \\ 0.0 &
                    \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \] </div>
        </li>
        <li>
            <strong>Laat Collection (\( \mathcal{R}_{\text{laat}} \)):</strong> Penalizes picking up cards due
            to card lead failures. Let \( e \in E_t \) be engine events during step \( t \). For each laat
            event, let \( C_e \) be the card count collected, and let \( \text{col}_e \) be the
            collector's absolute index (0 is the agent):
            <div class="equation">
                \[
                \mathcal{R}_{\text{laat}} = \sum_{e \in E_t, \text{type}(e)=\text{"laat"}} \begin{cases}
                -0.03 \cdot C_e & \text{if } \text{col}_e = 0 \quad \text{(Agent collected laat pile)} \\
                +0.01 \cdot C_e & \text{if } \text{col}_e \neq 0 \quad \text{(Opponent collected laat pile)}
                \end{cases}
                \]
            </div>
        </li>
        <li>
            <strong>Terminal Win/Loss (\( \mathcal{R}_{\text{terminal}} \)):</strong> Evaluated only at the
            final step. Let \( W \) be the set of players with the minimum hand size and \( L \) be the set of
            players with the maximum hand size:
            <div class="equation">
                \[
                \mathcal{R}_{\text{terminal}} = \begin{cases}
                +5.0 & \text{if } t \text{ is terminal and } 0 \in W \\
                -5.0 & \text{if } t \text{ is terminal and } 0 \in L \\
                0.0 & \text{otherwise}
                \end{cases}
                \]
            </div>
        </li>
    </ul>

    <p>
        By keeping intermediate shaping rewards small, we prevent the agent from optimizing for short-term card
        counts at the expense of overall victory.
    </p>

            Pretrained using Behavior Cloning on 50,000 heuristic decisions using a larger

            with a baseline understanding of card value. It developed a clean, high-card laat playing discipline
            (playing rank \( \ge 8 \) cards in laat situations 77.2% of the time), achieving a peak win rate of
            <code>0.947</code> in baseline tests and <code>0.857</code> in league testing.
        </li>
        <li>
            <strong>Model 3 (Robust League Agent):</strong>
            Pretrained on 200,000 decisions from Model 2 and trained for 800,000 steps using a <strong>League
                Pool</strong> of dynamic opponents. By playing against a shifting pool of intelligent,
            historical checkpoints, Model 3 learned to generalize and handle dynamic seating positions. While it
            slightly relaxed Model 2's strict high-card laat, it demonstrated much greater robustness, achieving a top
            standardized league win rate of <code>0.903</code>.
        </li>
        <li>
            <strong>Model 4 (Synthetic Multi-Teacher Experiment):</strong>
            Initialized from a multi-teacher dataset of 400,000 decisions using a categorical mixture: 45% Model
            3, 30% Model 2, 20% heuristic, and 5% random actions. It was trained for 1,000,000 steps against the

            laat discipline of Model 2, reaching a standardized league win rate of <code>0.890</code> and
            maintaining a 72.3% high-card laat rate.

    <h2 id="conclusion"><span class="header-num">6</span> Conclusion</h2>

    <h3 id="conclusion-eval-methods"><span class="header-num">6.1</span> Strategy Evaluation Methodologies</h3>
    <p>
        Evaluation methodologies extend far beyond standard win rates by employing specialized strategy scripts
        that measure card-use behavior, average final hand size, and specific reactions to being forced into
        giving laat. A composite score aggregates these metrics, explicitly favoring agents that win cleanly
        with fewer remaining cards and absolutely zero invalid actions.
    </p>

    <h3 id="conclusion-insights"><span class="header-num">6.2</span> Some key insights:</h3>
    <p>
        Development lifecycle yielded several key insights:
    </p>
    <ul>
        <li><strong>Action Masking:</strong> Essential for convergence in card games; without masking, the agent
            spends too much capacity exploring invalid card plays.</li>
        <li><strong>Warm Starts:</strong> Initializing PPO from behavior cloned weights dramatically accelerates
            training and guides the agent toward human-like card discipline.</li>
        <li><strong>League Training:</strong> Crucial for robustness, although it can dilute specific stylistic
            behaviors (like pure high-card laat play).</li>
    </ul>
